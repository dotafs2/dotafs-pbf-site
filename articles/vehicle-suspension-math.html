Vehicle Suspension and Tire Physics

How does a wheel discover the ground, support a car, turn torque into acceleration, and lose grip? A mathematical guide to the shared physics and three different game-vehicle implementations.

Input
Body pose, wheel geometry, road contact, controls
Output
Contact loads, tire forces, body motion
Comparisons
Chaos Vehicles · AVS · FGear
Reading time
About 24 minutes · three interactive labs

This is an implementation study, not a controlled handling benchmark. Equations marked teaching model are simplified explanatory models; implementation claims are grounded in the inspected source revisions identified at the end. No commercial source code is reproduced here.

01 / THE QUESTION

What must a vehicle simulation actually compute?

A rigid chassis alone can fall, bounce, and collide, but it cannot explain driving. At each wheel we need a contact state, a normal load from the suspension, longitudinal and lateral forces from the tire, and the opposing torque that changes wheel spin. Those forces act away from the center of mass, so they also produce pitch, roll, and yaw.

The useful abstraction is one rigid body plus four contact-and-wheel models. The tire is not simply a source of friction proportional to throttle: it can transmit only the forces allowed by its load, surface, slip, and chosen tire law. A suspension matters partly because it sets that normal load. “Better physics” therefore cannot be judged from spring motion alone.

Road geometry→Wheel contact→Spring load→Tire slip & forces→Chassis & wheel integration
STATE

Position p, orientation q, linear/angular velocity v, ω, and each wheel’s spin and suspension travel.

CONSTRAINT

The road can push on a wheel, not pull it into the road: normal load is nonnegative.

CONTROLS

Steering changes the local tire frame; engine and brakes change wheel torque, then slip and tire force.

BOUNDARY

This article concerns playable vehicle dynamics, not detailed deformation of each rubber tread block.

02 / CONTACT GEOMETRY

From a road query to one loaded wheel

Let aᵢ be wheel i’s chassis attachment, dᵢ its suspension direction, R its radius, and hᵢ the hit point reported by a road query. A ray or swept shape along dᵢ estimates the wheel center and the available suspension length ℓᵢ. Compression is xᵢ = clamp(ℓ₀ − ℓᵢ, 0, travel), where ℓ₀ is the uncompressed length. This is a geometric measurement, not a force yet.

Ray contacts are cheap and stable for smooth roads but a thin ray can miss a kerb beside the tire. A sphere/shape sweep samples a larger footprint but is costlier and still does not reproduce a deformable tire. A fully physical wheel is another choice: its collision body and constraints resolve contact instead of a single synthetic patch. Never confuse the visible wheel mesh with the contact representation.

Why the point of application matters

Two rigid-body equations · separate translations and rotationsm a = Σᵢ Fᵢ + m gI ω̇ + ω × (Iω) = Σᵢ(rᵢ × Fᵢ) + Σⱼτⱼ

The first equation uses an inertial (world) frame; the second uses axes fixed to the chassis. The two lines are separate equations, not a single product. Here m is mass; a = dv/dt is linear acceleration; ω̇ = dω/dt is angular acceleration in the chassis frame; I is the inertia tensor. Accelerations, forces, lever arms, and angular quantities shown in these equations are vectors.

1. Why is the translational equation m a = ΣF + mg?

Start with the momentum of a body with constant mass: P = m v, where v is the velocity of its center of mass. Newton’s law says the time derivative of momentum equals the net external force. Thus dP/dt = m dv/dt = m a. Separating gravity from the other forces gives m a = Σᵢ Fᵢ + m g. The vector g points downward; gravity is not also included in the sum. In a world frame with upward z, the vertical component is m az = Σᵢ Fi,z − mg, with the last g interpreted as the positive magnitude 9.81 m/s².

2. Why does an offset wheel force rotate the body?

Choose the center of mass as the moment origin. A force Fᵢ applied at displacement rᵢ creates torque rᵢ × Fᵢ; its magnitude is |rᵢ||Fᵢ| sin θ, so only the perpendicular lever arm matters. In a rear view with x pointing right and z up, a right-side force with r = (0.8, 0, 0) m and F = (0, 0, 1,000) N gives r × F = (0, −800, 0) N·m: the minus sign follows the chosen axes, while the roll-moment magnitude is 800 N·m. Pure applied couples, if any, are added as τⱼ. An equal and opposite left–right pair can give zero net vertical force while creating a roll moment. The point rᵢ must be where the chosen rigid body actually receives the force: a chassis attachment for a separately simulated wheel, or an effective contact point in a simplified chassis model.

3. Where does ω × (Iω) come from?

Angular momentum about the center of mass is H = Iω, and the angular-momentum law in the world frame is dH/dt|world = Σ torque. To keep the inertia tensor I constant, express H and ω in axes rotating with the chassis. The transport rule is dH/dt|world = dH/dt|body + ω × H. With a rigid body and constant I in those axes, dH/dt|body = I ω̇; substituting H = Iω gives the second equation. Express all forces, lever arms, and torques on its right-hand side in the same chassis frame. Uniform gravity acts at the center of mass, so it contributes no moment about that point. In principal axes where I = diag(Iₓ,Iᵧ,Iz), the first component is Iₓ ω̇ₓ + (Iz − Iᵧ)ωᵧωz = Mₓ. The second term shows how simultaneous angular velocities couple even without a new torque about x. For rotation about a fixed principal axis the cross term vanishes, leaving the familiar scalar I ω̇ = τ. For combined roll, pitch, and yaw it generally cannot be dropped.

In a game step, these equations yield a = (ΣF + mg)/m in world coordinates and ω̇ = I⁻¹[Σ(rᵢ × Fᵢ) + Στⱼ − ω × (Iω)] in chassis coordinates. Transform forces into the appropriate frame before each calculation, then integrate linear and angular velocity over the physics time step. Moving the same force vector to another point leaves a unchanged but can change roll angular acceleration.

03 / SPRING & DAMPER

Ride height, oscillation, and load transfer

A linear spring creates force proportional to compression. A damper resists speed of compression or rebound; it is not another spring and does not determine the final static ride height. With x positive in compression, an idealized support-force magnitude is Fs = max(0, kx + c ẋ), where k is in N/m, c in N·s/m, and ẋ in m/s. The tire normal load Fz is related to this support force but need not equal its magnitude on banked ground, with force offsets, load blending, or a separate constraint. Some implementations apply support along the suspension axis and others along the road normal.

At rest on level ground, a symmetric four-wheel vehicle has approximately Fz ≈ mg/4 per wheel; therefore x₀ ≈ mg/(4k) if the springs are identical and there is no preload. If m = 1200 kg and k = 30,000 N/m per wheel, x₀ ≈ 0.098 m. The spring travel must leave room both to compress further over a bump and to extend into a dip.

One-corner teaching modelmₛ ÿ + c(ẏ − ṙ) + k(y − r) = 0

mₛ is the supported corner mass, y its displacement from static equilibrium, and r road displacement. Natural frequency fₙ = √(k/mₛ)/(2π); critical damping c₍crit₎ = 2√(kmₛ); damping ratio ζ = c/c₍crit₎. A one-mass model does not represent unsprung wheel mass, tire compliance, bump stops, or an anti-roll bar.

INTERACTIVE 01 / BUMP RESPONSE

What happens when stiffness or damping changes?

Apply a small road step to one corner. Compare body height, road height, and the instantaneous support force.

Teaching model: a mass–spring–damper in continuous contact. The step’s instantaneous road velocity is ignored; impacts and tire deformation require a richer model.

A more complete quarter-car has sprung mass mₛ, wheel/axle mass mᵤ, suspension kₛ,cₛ, and tire stiffness kₜ. In one sign convention: mₛ z̈ₛ = −kₛ(zₛ−zᵤ)−cₛ(żₛ−żᵤ) and mᵤ z̈ᵤ = kₛ(zₛ−zᵤ)+cₛ(żₛ−żᵤ)−kₜ(zᵤ−r), after gravity is removed by measuring displacement from equilibrium. It predicts both body bounce and wheel hop; a single ray wheel approximates or omits the latter.

Load transfer is a torque balance, not extra weight

In a turn, lateral acceleration aᵧ at center-of-mass height h creates roll moment approximately m aᵧ h. With track width t, transferring load Δ from the inside side to the outside side creates opposing moment Δt, so Δ ≈ m aᵧ h/t for the entire car under a simple rigid, flat-road assumption. Total vertical load is still roughly mg: outside tires gain what inside tires lose. Front and rear load-transfer shares depend on roll stiffness, geometry, and unsprung effects; this formula alone cannot allocate the transfer to each axle.

During straight-line acceleration or braking, a first approximation for load transferred between axles is Δfront↔rear ≈ m aₓ h/L, where L is wheelbase. An anti-roll bar couples left and right suspension travel: a schematic axle model applies opposing increments Fbar,L = kbar(xL − xR) and Fbar,R = −Fbar,L, with signs chosen for the local axes. It changes the distribution of load, not total grip for free. Many tires exhibit load sensitivity: doubling normal load produces less than double peak lateral force, so an uneven load split can reduce axle grip.

Effective tire loadingFz,inside + Fz,outside ≈ axle load   ·   Fbar,L + Fbar,R = 0

This balance assumes no aerodynamic downforce change, road bank, curb impact, or vertical chassis acceleration. A suspension force may point along the suspension axis or the road normal; the tire law should use its actual normal component.

SEPARATE THE ROLES

Stiffness primarily sets static compression and body frequency. Damping sets transient oscillation. Travel, bump stops, anti-roll coupling, center of mass, and tire contact determine whether the wheel stays loaded.

04 / TIRE FORCES

Why a tire needs slip to make force

At the contact patch, let Vx be forward ground-relative speed, Vy sideways speed, Ω wheel angular speed, and R effective radius. A common pedagogical longitudinal slip ratio is κ = (RΩ − Vx)/max(|Vx|, vₑ); slip angle is α = atan2(Vy, max(|Vx|, vₑ)). Here vₑ is a low-speed regularizer. Different implementations use different denominators, clamping, and sign conventions, so these are shared concepts, not a claimed literal formula from every codebase.

Positive drive torque tends to raise Ω, producing κ > 0; braking lowers it. Steering changes the wheel’s local frame, creating α even while the chassis moves forward. The tire force is a function of slip, normal load, surface, camber, and sometimes pressure and speed: (Fx,Fy,Mz) = T(κ,α,Fz,γ,p,V). Mz is self-aligning torque. A game tire model may omit some arguments; no universal single “correct” tire curve exists.

Simple shared limit · teaching model√[(Fx/(μx Fz))² + (Fy/(μy Fz))²] ≤ 1

A friction ellipse is a useful budget model: using more force to accelerate leaves less available to turn. Actual tire data may have asymmetric, load-dependent peaks and non-elliptical combined-slip behavior. On no contact, Fz = 0 and this tire-force budget vanishes.

Teaching model: isotropic circle, fixed Fz. Not the exact combined-slip law of any one implementation.

Close to zero slip, the tire’s initial lateral slope is cornering stiffness Cα = |∂Fy/∂α|₍α≈0₎, measured in N/rad. Near the peak, the response saturates; after the peak, some fitted tires lose force as slip grows. Load sensitivity, camber, pressure, road material, temperature, and wetness can shift the curve. Transient tire response can be modeled with a relaxation length λ: approximately τ ≈ λ/max(|Vx|,vₑ) and τ Ḟy + Fy = Fy,steady(α). This prevents an instantaneous jump to the steady-state curve; whether a particular game implementation actually models it must be checked separately.

For a driven wheel, an idealized angular equation is Iʷ Ω̇ = Tdrive − Tbrake − Fx R − Trolling. Newton’s third law couples the ground force to wheel spin. Integrating wheel rotation solely from chassis speed would hide wheelspin and wheel lock. At almost zero speed, avoid dividing by Vx = 0; low-speed blending or a static-friction treatment is necessary.

05 / THREE IMPLEMENTATIONS

What mathematics does each system choose?

The core laws are shared; what differs is how contact is represented, how spring load is coupled to the chassis, and especially how slip becomes tire force. These observations describe the inspected source, not the full set of possible configurations or a ranking of measured realism.

A / CHAOS VEHICLES

Spring load plus a grip-limited wheel

  1. Contact. Each wheel queries a road along its suspension axis; the inspected path supports raycasts and sphere sweeps. The chassis carries suspension targets into a constraint path, with a direct-force fallback.
  2. Vertical force. A displacement-based spring uses Fspring = kx and two damping coefficients selected by the direction of displacement change, of the form Fdamp = −c ẋ under the code’s displacement sign. Normal load can blend computed spring force with a resting-force setting. An anti-roll calculation couples the two wheels on an axle.
  3. Tire. Drive/brake torque divided by radius proposes longitudinal force. Lateral force comes from cornering stiffness or a lateral-slip graph. A circle-like clamp scales the joint force when √(Fx²+Fy²) > μ Fz, with additional low-speed and assist logic.
  4. Trade-off. Easy to tune through direct grip and steering quantities; the inspected default tire path is not a calibrated Pacejka law. Constraint-based suspension and force fallback should not be conflated.

B / ADVANCED VEHICLE SYSTEM (AVS 1.4.3)

Custom slip dynamics, with two wheel modes

  1. Contact. The inspected vehicle code line-traces from a compressed-wheel start to an extended-wheel end, subtracts wheel diameter from the hit distance, and clamps spring length.
  2. Vertical force. It explicitly converts configured spring rate from N/mm to N/m and compression from cm to m; the force is k·compression + c·compression speed. In this path it applies the force along the hit normal. Excess-compression handling adds an extra support term.
  3. Raycast tire mode. It computes contact-plane forward/right axes; a target longitudinal slip is driven by torque versus Fz R μx, lateral target slip blends a low-speed sign response with slip angle, and both slips are interpolated over time. A normalized combined-slip vector and a square-root lateral shaping turn Fz μx and Fz μy into force.
  4. Physical wheel mode. A wheel collision body receives the opposite suspension force. Contact friction and drive torque then rely on the rigid-body contact solution instead of the preceding synthetic tire-force branch. These modes should not be described as one tire law.

C / FGEAR VEHICLE PHYSICS

Spring preload plus interchangeable tire curves

  1. Contact. The inspected wheel code can use ray contacts or shape-based detection and computes wheel travel from the hit geometry. The contact normal participates in spring force.
  2. Vertical force. Configurable up/down travel, preload, spring rate, and separate compression/relaxation dampers produce load; the effective damping coefficient depends on the direction of travel. An optional suspension-limit constraint can prevent excessive chassis penetration.
  3. Slip. Longitudinal slip starts from RΩ − Vx; slip angle starts from atan(Vy/(|Vx|+ε)). Ratios and angles are bounded near difficult low-speed cases. The tire object receives load, slip, camber, pressure, radius, and speed.
  4. Tire choices. The inspected Simple tire uses a load-scaled sine/arctangent curve; Tire96 and MF61 include load-dependent Magic Formula terms and combined-slip adjustments. MF61 additionally includes pressure and moment terms. These are model families, not proof that a bundled parameter set matches any measured tire.
Shape of a fitted tire curveF(s) = D sin{ C atan[ B s − E(B s − atan(B s)) ] }

This schematic Magic Formula explains FGear’s Simple tire family: s is slip (ratio or angle), B controls slope, C shape, D peak, and E curvature. The detailed 1996/MF6.1 families add many load, camber, pressure, shift, and combined-slip coefficients. This is not the exact equation used for Chaos or AVS.

07 / BUILDING A GAME CAR

A practical simulation loop and tuning order

  1. 01 · Establish units and chassis state

    Use consistent mass, length, force, torque, time, and inertia units. Put the center of mass and inertia tensor in a plausible location. Start with a fixed physics step.

  2. 02 · Measure contact

    For every wheel: determine contact, hit normal/position, suspension length, road-relative velocity at the hit, and surface properties. A contact point on a moving platform must use relative velocity.

  3. 03 · Compute suspension

    Set travel and spring rate from static sag; choose damping from a target fraction of 2√(km). Limit compression at bump stops and set load to zero when airborne. Add left–right anti-roll coupling only after individual wheels work.

  4. 04 · Compute wheel spin and tires

    Transform contact velocity into each steered wheel frame; compute slip with a low-speed rule, evaluate tire forces from the chosen model, enforce combined-slip behavior, then update Ω using drive/brake/contact torques.

  5. 05 · Apply and integrate

    Apply force at each contact or prescribed suspension point; integrate rigid-body linear and angular motion. Use substeps when wheel rotation, contact, or stiff springs change faster than the render frame. Interpolate graphics separately from physics.

  6. 06 · Instrument before adding assists

    Plot per-wheel compression, Fz, κ, α, Fx, Fy, angular speed, and stability-control interventions. Tune the bare car before ABS, traction control, camera shake, or visual wheel motion.

Processor workCPU: contact queries → wheel/tire updates → rigid-body solve   |   GPU: chiefly rendering

These inspected implementations calculate their core forces in processor-side simulation code; a GPU draws the result and may compute unrelated visual effects. A GPU-based vehicle solver is possible, but is not implied by high visual quality. Fixed-step cost scales approximately with wheel count, contact-query complexity, constraint iterations, and substeps—not only the number of tire coefficients.

Source inspection confirms specific algorithms but does not provide calibrated real-world error, equal-hardware runtime, tire test data, or a controlled comparison among the three. Formula-rich FGear settings can be physically motivated yet still wrong without fitting; simplified AVS or Chaos settings can be more convincing for a target game after careful tuning. All three abstract away distributed rubber deformation, temperature, wear, and many contact-patch effects. Contact query choices can fail on sharp geometry; numerical substeps and solver constraints influence stability.

DECISION RULE

Choose the contact representation and tire law according to the phenomenon you need to reproduce, then validate the same maneuvers and telemetry under the same conditions. Evaluate physical behavior and control feel separately.
